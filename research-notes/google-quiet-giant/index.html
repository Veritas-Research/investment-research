Veritas Research · October 31, 2026

Google fell 30% from its high. Is Search breaking, or is the market reading the wrong number?

The views and analysis here are my own and have nothing to do with my employer. This is research, not investment advice. I hold broad index funds, and no direct positions in any company named here. Some of the numbers that matter most are not published by Alphabet, so several figures below are estimates, and I label them as estimates. Treat the projections as ranges in the right ballpark, not as forecasts to the decimal.

Summary: in two minutes

  • The stock had a terrible week. Alphabet fell 12.6% the day after its third quarter report, to $283.82, and closed the week at $279.84. That is about 30% below its May high of $402.62 (Verified) [5].
  • The headlines said "miss." Most of the miss was accounting. Earnings per share came in at $2.12 against about $3.00 expected, but an $8.7 billion loss on investments Alphabet holds did most of that damage. It has nothing to do with how Google's businesses are running (Verified) [1].
  • Search is not breaking. It is slowing. Search revenue still grew 12% to $63.4 billion, down from 19% growth in the first quarter. Google says searches are growing at the fastest pace in five years. One issue Google points to is that AI Mode earns less for now (Verified for the revenue figures [1]; Secondhand for the AI Mode point [3]).
  • The quiet giant is Cloud. Google Cloud doubled to $30.7 billion in the quarter, at a 36% operating margin, with $561 billion of signed future business. Compared with a year ago, Cloud added more profit ($7.5 billion) than Search, YouTube and subscriptions combined ($5.6 billion) (Verified) [1]. The $561 billion of signed future business comes from the call (Secondhand) [3].
  • The real problem is cash, not the business. Alphabet spent $51.4 billion on data centers and chips in the quarter. Its free cash flow (cash left after that spending) was negative for a second quarter in a row. It has raised about $62 billion of new stock (including about $19 billion of preferred shares that convert into common stock) and about $85 billion of new debt this year, and bought back no stock (Verified) [1] [7].
  • Does all this data center spending pay back? I built a model of $100 billion of spending over a five year chip life. On rented Nvidia style economics it roughly earns its cost of capital (about 9% a year). On Google's own cheaper chips (TPUs) it earns about 20% a year. A payback in one or two years would need revenue no public source supports (Estimate) [45].
  • Take away. The market is pricing a cash crunch as if it were a broken business. Google is not broken. Whether it is cheap depends on two things the next few months will show: how good its own chips really are, and whether 2027 spending can be paid for without more outside money.

What is going on

In one week, the story on Google flipped from "AI winner" to "AI victim." The argument goes like this: people ask chatbots instead of searching, so Search ads shrink, and meanwhile Google is pouring hundreds of billions into data centers it has to borrow for.

This is half true. So what does the whole picture look like?

What I found surprised me a little. The part everyone is worried about (Search) is holding up better than the headlines suggest. The part nobody is talking about (Cloud) is the biggest change in Google's business in a decade. And the part that should worry you (cash) is real, but it is a question of timing and funding, not of a business falling apart.

A restaurant analogy

I love analogies; they help me picture complex subjects. Here's the one I'll use throughout.

Search is the flagship restaurant. It is still packed. More people walk in every year. But some customers now order the new tasting menu (AI Mode), which right now brings in less per table than the old menu.

Cloud is the second location. It opened recently, it's already full every night, and it has years of reservations booked. It is also expensive to build: new kitchens, new ovens, new staff.

Capex is the construction bill. Capex (capital expenditure) is money spent on long lasting things like buildings and chips. While the second location is being built, the owner's bank account looks scary, even if both restaurants are busy.

YouTube is the patio. Lots of people sit there. It earns money, but nowhere near what it could if it were run as well as the competitor's patio across the street (Meta).

The market looked at the bank account and panicked. This piece looks at the restaurants.

Part 2Is Search breaking?

No. It is slowing, and the reason matters.

The growth path

Quarter (2026) Search revenue Growth Label
Q1 $60.4 billion 19.1% Verified [4]
Q2 $63.3 billion 16.8% Verified [4]
Q3 $63.4 billion 12.0% Verified [1]

Search still added $6.8 billion of revenue compared with a year ago. That is a business still growing, not shrinking. But the growth rate dropped almost five points in one quarter, and that is what spooked people.

Why it slowed, according to Google

  • Currency was the biggest factor. Chief Business Officer Philipp Schindler called it "the single largest driver of Search deceleration relative to Q2" (Secondhand, call transcript) [3].
  • AI Mode earns less per search, for now. Management said growth in AI Mode queries weighed on revenue "because of the lower monetization we were seeing in AI Mode," and it is rolling out ads inside AI Mode in more countries (Secondhand) [3].
  • Other one time factors: weaker Asia Pacific retail ads after the EU ended a duty exemption on small imports, the end of the World Cup boost, and a tough comparison with late 2025 (Secondhand) [3].
  • Searches are up, not down. Sundar Pichai said query growth is "the highest it's been in the last five years." He gave no number (Secondhand) [3].

Nobody has published how much of the slowdown is currency and how much is AI Mode. That is the honest gap.

Are people leaving Google for chatbots?

  • Not in the data I could find. Similarweb found that about 95% of ChatGPT users also use Google, and that Google draws about 3.3 billion monthly visitors against about 655 million for all AI chatbots combined (Reported) [10]. Their conclusion: AI is layering on top of Google, not replacing it.
  • Publishers are the ones getting hurt. Ahrefs reports that top ranking pages see a 58% lower average click rate when an AI Overview appears (Secondhand, company press release) [11]. That hurts websites. It is not the same thing as Google's ad revenue falling.
  • Ad clicks are still growing. An advertiser data panel showed Google search ad clicks up 9% and price per click up 4% in the third quarter (Secondhand; panel data, not Alphabet's) [12].

Back to the restaurant: more customers are walking in than ever. Some of them are ordering the tasting menu, which earns less per table today. The question is whether Google learns to price the tasting menu properly. It has every incentive to.

Part 3The quiet giant

Cloud is already covering the Search slowdown, in revenue and in profit.

  • A year ago, Cloud was about one dollar in every seven. Now it is about one in four.
  • Of every new dollar Alphabet earned, about 63 cents came from Cloud. Search brought about 27 cents.
  • At today's pace, Cloud is a $123 billion a year business, about half the size of Search ($253 billion a year). A year ago it was running at about $61 billion a year (Estimate from Verified figures) [1].

Cloud in numbers

  • Revenue: $30.7 billion, up 103% (Verified) [1]. Growth has accelerated all year: about 63% in the first quarter, 82% in the second (Verified) [4].
  • Profit: $11.1 billion of operating income, a 36% margin, up from 24% a year ago (Verified) [1]. The rest of Google runs at about 41%, so Cloud is now almost as profitable per dollar.
  • Reservations: $561 billion of signed future business, with just over 55% due within two years (Secondhand, call transcript) [3].
  • Capacity: Pichai says Google is working "to bring more capacity online to meet this long term demand," and Cloud chief Thomas Kurian said "we could obviously serve a lot more demand with capacity" (Secondhand) [3]. The second location is full.
  • For comparison: Microsoft Azure grew 48% and Amazon Web Services 39% in the same quarter (Verified) [17] [18].

The profit swap

Same idea for profit. Google as a whole is still growing. One part is slowing, and another part is more than making up for it.

Here's what took me a minute to fully appreciate:

  • Cloud went from about 10% of Google's business profit to about 22% in one year.
  • Cloud added $7.5 billion of profit, more than all of Google Services combined ($5.6 billion).
  • The Search slowdown cost roughly $2.7 billion of revenue and about $1.1 billion of profit compared with growing at the second quarter's pace (Estimate). Cloud's gain covers that about seven times over.
  • One honest offset: the costs Alphabet does not assign to any business, mostly AI research, rose $3.2 billion and ate part of Cloud's gain.

What if Search actually starts shrinking?

This is what the bears are constantly saying (and have been since AI really took off) so I tested it. Search makes about $103 billion a year of operating profit at the margin of Google's services business (Estimate; Alphabet does not report Search profit on its own). Cloud makes about $44 billion a year at today's pace (Estimate from Verified figures) [1].

Cloud profit grew about 200% in the last year. Even a 20% drop in Search would be covered if Cloud keeps growing at a fraction of today's pace. So on profit, Cloud can make up the shortfall. On cash, it is a different story. That is Part 4.

Where this is weakest: a large share of those reservations comes from a few AI labs. Anthropic alone reportedly committed about $200 billion over five years, more than 40% of Google's backlog at the time (Reported) [19]. UBS reportedly estimates OpenAI and Anthropic at 27% of Cloud revenue in 2026 and more than 48% in 2027, as cited by Ed Zitron on Bloomberg (Secondhand, no method published) [20]. Google is also an investor in Anthropic, so part of the demand is circular. If one big customer stumbles, the reservation book shrinks.

The catch: Cloud only grows this fast because Google keeps building data centers and buying chips. Revenue and profit are the easy half of the story. The harder half is whether all that spending ever pays back. That is Parts 4 and 5.

Part 4The bill

This is what the market is actually punishing.

Stock buybacks $0 $0 Verified [1]
  • How it's being paid for. In June, Alphabet announced about $80 billion of new stock: $30 billion sold through banks, a $40 billion program to sell shares gradually, and a $10 billion sale to Berkshire Hathaway at about $350 a share (Reported) [7]. It actually raised about $50 billion in the second quarter (part of it preferred shares that convert into common stock) and $12.4 billion more in the third. Long term debt went from $46.5 billion to $125.1 billion in nine months, on about $85 billion of new borrowing (Verified) [1] [4], including a bond sale of up to $25 billion in August (Reported) [8].
  • A fair note: Alphabet says the $40 billion program is "primarily intended to be used to meet tax obligations associated with employee equity grants," not to fund data centers (Verified) [4]. It still sold $12.4 billion of stock under that program in the third quarter, with $27.6 billion left, while buying none back, so shareholders are being diluted (Verified) [1].
  • The balance sheet is still strong. Alphabet holds $265 billion of cash and investments against $125 billion of long term debt, about $140 billion net (Verified) [1].
  • The bill is getting bigger. The 2026 capex plan is $195 to $205 billion. For 2027, management only said capex will "increase significantly." There is no dollar figure yet; the "above $300 billion" number in headlines does not appear in the call (Secondhand) [3].
  • Depreciation is catching up. Depreciation (the yearly cost of wearing out buildings and chips) was $8.4 billion in the third quarter, up 49% from a year ago (Verified) [1]. That is the construction bill showing up in profit, and it keeps rising.

Best way to picture this is to go back to the restaurant analogy: both locations are full. But the owner is paying for construction with a loan and by selling part of the business, and has stopped buying back shares from the existing owners. You can see why the bank manager is nervous, even if dinner service is going great.

Part 5Does $100 billion of AI spending pay back?

This is the real test and question. And to test this out, I built a model to find out. The short answer: it depends on whose chips.

How the model works

Imagine spending $100 billion once, today, on chips and data centers. Then:

  1. The chips earn rent. Each dollar of spending brings in some amount of revenue a year once it is running. This is the single most important number.
  2. Rent falls as chips age. Newer chips come out, so older ones rent for less each year.
  3. Power and upkeep don't fall. The electricity bill stays the same even as rent drops.
  4. After five years the chips retire. The buildings, power and cooling (about 40% of the spending, by Alphabet's own split (Reported) [29]) last much longer and can host the next generation of chips.

I calibrated the base case so the second year margin lands near Google Cloud's real 36% (Estimate) [45].

How much revenue does a dollar of spending earn?

This is the number that decides everything, and nobody publishes it. Here is every estimate I could find:

All from my model [45]. The cost of capital I compare against is about 9%.

Three things stood out to me.

  1. The base case is break even. 8.6% against a 9% cost of capital. Spending on rented style economics roughly earns what the money costs, no more.
  2. Google's own chips are the whole story. Google designs its own AI chips, called TPUs (Tensor Processing Units). SemiAnalysis estimates they cost Google about 44% less than Nvidia's comparable GB200 system, and 30% to 50% less for outside customers (Secondhand) [21]. On cost per million tokens the edge is narrower: about 19% cheaper than Nvidia's B200 at one speed setting and about 8% at another (Reported) [22]. My Google TPU case uses a 30% saving, which rests on the older comparison against the GB200 system [21]. At that saving, the return goes from break even to about 20% a year. That is the difference between a cash crunch and a good investment.
  3. A one or two year payback is not realistic. You would need about $1.50 of revenue a year for every dollar spent. The best figure I found is $1.14, and that one is a short term contract price.

The shape of the cash surprised me

I expected years four and five to be the best. It's the opposite.

Year two is the best year, because the chips are fully running and rent has barely fallen. After that rent keeps falling while the power bill doesn't. In the base case, the chips alone only just pay for themselves (about 0.7% a year). The profit comes from the buildings, which keep their value and host the next round of chips.

What it means for Alphabet's cash

If capex grows 40% in 2027 and then slows, this kind of spending only starts producing cash on its own in about 2032 in the base case, 2031 with TPU economics, and 2030 in the bull case. In the bear case, not before 2035 (Estimate) [45]. Until then, Search pays the bill. That is why negative free cash flow can last for years without meaning the business is failing.

Is Google stretching the life of its servers?

This matters because a longer life means lower yearly depreciation and higher reported profit.

  • Google's policy: "We depreciate servers and network equipment generally over a period of six years" (Verified) [9]. It moved from four to six years in 2023, which cut 2023 depreciation by about $3.9 billion (Reported) [30].
  • Peers: Microsoft and Oracle also use six years, and Meta uses 5.5 (Secondhand) [46]. Amazon shortened some servers from six to five years in January 2025, citing "an increased pace of technology development, particularly in the area of artificial intelligence and machine learning" (Reported) [31].
  • What the research papers say: I found no study showing modern AI chips wearing out within five or six years, but none follows them that long either. Meta's Llama 3 training run saw 466 interruptions in 54 days on up to 16,384 H100 chips, 419 of them unexpected; about 78% of those were confirmed hardware issues, with GPUs the largest category at 58.7% (Verified, preprint) [32]. A 2.5 year university study of about 1,056 A100 and H100 chips found six replacements (four A100, two H100) and no clear trend in error rates once the systems settled into normal operation, though H100 memory proved less resilient than A100 (Verified, preprint) [34]. The often quoted "AI chips last one to three years" traces back to one unnamed Google architect, quoted through Tom's Hardware, who gave no failure data; TrendForce itself says it "cannot be considered 100% accurate" (Reported) [36]. From my own anecdotal use and evidence, I still use older A100 and H100 for my side work and experiments, and they are very much still in demand and constantly not available (but take that with a grain of salt).
  • What happens in practice: Amazon's cloud chief says AWS has "never retired an A100 server," a chip from 2020 (Reported) [37]. Google's Amin Vahdat says Google's "seven and eight-year-old TPUs have 100 percent utilization" (Reported) [38]. Both are interested parties, speaking during a shortage.
  • The critics: Michael Burry argues that big tech overstates how long its hardware lasts and understates depreciation by about $176 billion from 2026 to 2028 (Opinion) [39]. The article does not give his method, and he is betting against AI stocks. My own personal experience (see above) is counter to this; so yes, I am taking a stance against Michael (whom I deeply respect by the way).

My take: six years looks reasonable while computing power is scarce, and Google's own TPUs support a longer life than rented Nvidia chips. It is at the long end of peers, and it would look stretched if the shortage ends and rent on older chips falls. In my model, moving from five to six years only adds about one point of return. Rent and revenue per dollar matter far more than the depreciation schedule. And until we have some major shifts in chip architecture, or AI that needs different types of compute, I can see this being the case for at least the near to medium future (3 to 5 years).

Meta has two giant feeds where AI decides which ad you see next: Facebook and Instagram. Google doesn't have a social network. YouTube is the closest thing it has to Instagram, and it is a big one:

  • YouTube had a record 15.8% of all US TV viewing time in August, about double Netflix (Secondhand, Nielsen) [13]. More than a third of YouTube ad revenue now comes from the living room TV (Secondhand) [3].
  • But YouTube ad growth has slowed: about 11%, 13% and then 9% across 2026, and WARC Media forecasts about 7% for the full year (Verified [1] [4]; forecast Reported [14]).
  • Subscriptions are growing faster than ads. YouTube Music and Premium passed 150 million subscribers including trials (Verified, company slides) [2].

What would have to be true for YouTube to become the next channel:

  • Google shows a company wide lift in conversions or ad prices from AI, the way Meta does.
  • YouTube ad growth gets back above 15%.
  • Ads inside AI Mode and Gemini start earning close to classic search.

None of these has happened yet. I treat YouTube as an option on the upside, not part of my base case. This is my opinion.

My thoughts going forward

So based on all this information, here is what I think.

  • Search is slowing, not shrinking. People haven't left. The open question is whether Google learns to charge properly for AI answers. I think it will, but I can't prove it yet.
  • Cloud is the turning point. It already adds more profit than everything else Google does combined. On profit, it can cover even a sharp Search decline.
  • Cash is the real risk, and it's a timing problem. The spending only pays back on a four to five year view, and only well if Google's own chips are as cheap as estimated. Until then, Search pays the bill and Alphabet keeps raising money.
  • The price assumes very little. At about 19 times next year's earnings, Alphabet is priced like an average company, not like one with a cloud business doubling.
  • Where I could be wrong: if the TPU cost edge is much smaller than estimated, the spending is roughly break even. If 2027 capex comes in far above what cash flow can cover, more dilution follows. If Anthropic or another big customer stumbles, a large part of the Cloud reservation book is at risk.
  • What I would not claim: that Alphabet will hit any particular price, or that Search revenue can't fall. I'm saying the market is reacting to the bank account, and I think it's underweighting the restaurants.

Methodology and sources

I looked at company filings and earnings releases, call transcripts, financial journals, data vendors and research papers myself. To bring it together I used AI agents to fetch and organise the material, and separate AI assisted verification passes to reopen the key numbers at their source. Twenty nine load bearing figures were rechecked against their original documents; about a third needed a correction or a caveat. Before publishing, every source below was reopened and every claim that cites it was checked again; about forty claims and citations were corrected or reworded. The corrected versions are used above.
