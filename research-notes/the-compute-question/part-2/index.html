The Compute Question

The views and analysis here are my own and have nothing to do with my employer. This is research, not investment advice. I hold broad market index funds, which include several companies named here; I hold no direct positions in them. Many of the numbers that matter most are not published by the companies involved, so several figures below are estimates, and I label them as estimates. Where a named news outlet reported something I could not check against the underlying document, I say so and name the outlet. Treat the projections as ranges in the right ballpark, not as forecasts to the decimal.

Summary: in one minute

  • Renting AI chips on the open market got more expensive in 2026, after getting cheaper on most price indexes in 2025. At one large provider, an H100, a chip first sold in 2022, went from $2.99 to $3.99 an hour on its list. Some of that may be scarcity, some cost pass through, and some capacity moving into long contracts; big cloud contract prices did not rise.
  • Chips are arriving faster than powered buildings. That is real, and it explains a story going around about chips sitting in warehouses. But nobody publishes how many chips are waiting for power. My rough estimate is about 4 gigawatts worldwide, within a range of zero to about 12. That range cannot rule out the large dollar figures going around, but it cannot confirm them either.
  • There are two physical limitations that holding things back. Powered buildings (grid connections, turbines, transformers) limit where chips can run. Memory limits how many chips can be built. Micron's chief executive says more than three quarters of its 2027 output is already committed.
  • The evidence I can see says 2027 is tight at the margin. Based on models I built, the central case (at today's adoption pace) says things will get a bit constricted (think a highway getting a little fuller than open roads; or more specifically from about 60% to about 65%, but not jammed). A real squeeze in 2027 mostly needs supply to not come online.
  • 2028 is where this all can change. If the chips and buildings ordered in 2026 and 2027 arrive, and if usage speeds up to about 5 times a year, it reaches a shortage in 2028. At today's pace (about 4 times a year), the road stays about two thirds full. If usage or funding stumbles, 2028 is when a glut would show.
  • Major determining factors: how much AI capacity is really running today, how much work each megawatt really does, and whether the two AI labs at the centre of the order books keep paying. None of these is published so we've had to make assumptions, and did so by looking at some potential ranges.

1. Capacity: using a highway analogy

I love analogies; they help me picture complex subjects in a way that is easier to understand. Here is an analogy using cars and highways that I hope most people will understand.

  • Cars are the demand. Every question to an AI and every step an AI agent takes is traffic, counted in tokens (a token is about three quarters of a word).
  • Lanes are the supply. Data centers, chips and the electricity to run them. A new lane takes years: land, power lines, turbines, transformers, and the memory chips that go inside each processor.
  • Smaller cars are efficiency. Better chips and software fit more traffic into the same lane.

A jam happens when traffic grows faster than lanes plus smaller cars combined. That is the whole question this piece tries to answer for 2027 and 2028.

3. The public price signal: what it costs to rent a chip

I believe in efficient and informed markets (most of the time). If the world were swimming in compute, renting it would get cheaper. Anyone with a credit card can rent AI chips by the hour from providers like RunPod and Lambda, so their price lists are a public test. They are a small slice of the market: most compute sits inside the big clouds and AI labs under long contracts. So read this section as a signal, not a census.

What this does and does not tell us

  • 2025 looked like the start of a glut on the price indexes, though Lambda's list prices did not move. 2026 reversed that, at more than one provider and on more than one index.
  • Rising prices on old chips can mean three things, and all three are probably at work (and can be true at the same time):
    1. Scarcity: usable, powered compute is short.
    2. Cost pass through: memory prices rose sharply in early 2026 (high bandwidth memory (HBM) contract prices for 2026 deliveries reportedly about 20%, Secondhand; ordinary memory forecast to rise far more, Estimate) [25], and the grid auction above raises power capacity prices from mid 2027. CoreWeave's finance chief said it is passing through component price increases (Secondhand, call transcript) [20]. This fits new chips better than old ones: an H100 already in a rack costs no more to run because new memory got dearer.
    3. Fewer chips on the open market: providers moving capacity into long contracts, which leaves less to rent by the hour.
  • What I can say with confidence: the open rental market did not get cheaper in 2026, which is hard to square with a glut today.
  • What I cannot say: that the whole fleet is full. Big cloud contract prices were flat in March and April and have since eased to about $7.17 (Silicon Data) [21], and transacted prices for older chips (Ornn's A100 at $1.09) [24] sit far below list prices.

4. The warehouse question

A popular claim and story making the rounds right now (Oct 2026) is that the big cloud companies are buying chips they cannot install. Writer Ed Zitron has argued that more than half of Nvidia's recent chips may be sitting unused [31]. Here is what holds up.

What is real

  • Business Insider reported on 20 October, citing internal emails and an internal meeting, that Amazon expected about $5 billion of Nvidia's newest racks in August and about $3 billion a month after that, taking ownership earlier than before. Some sites had space but not enough power, so racks were staged in warehouses until the buildings were ready. Amazon called it a best business practice (Reported) [26].
  • Microsoft's chief executive said on a podcast in October 2025 that he may have chips in inventory he cannot plug in, for lack of finished, powered buildings (Reported, on the record) [27]. Microsoft has since said it roughly halved the time from delivery to live service for new chips (Secondhand, call summary).
  • Bloomberg reported in September that Microsoft has about 12 gigawatts of total capacity, of which about 2 gigawatts is built around AI chips (Reported) [28].
  • The Guardian reported in August, from an internal document, that Microsoft had about 2.2 million AI chips installed. Microsoft said the estimates drew the wrong conclusions from incorrect assumptions. The article was written jointly with Ed Zitron, so it is not independent support for his wider claim (Reported) [29].
  • Microsoft reportedly pulled out of a UK site planned for about 23,000 of Nvidia's newest chips after the local network operator said power would not arrive until the 2030s. The developer, Nscale, says it remains committed to the project and is looking at other power options (Reported, the Telegraph via the Register) [30].

What does not hold up

  • Zitron's figure of $200 billion to $300 billion of chips "in warehouses or sitting in unpowered data centres" rests on assumed splits (for example, half of capital spending going to chips). He calls his range "entirely speculative". It is not a count (Estimate) [31].
  • The often repeated figure of 15 gigawatts of 2027 chips that cannot come online comes from a social media post by Elon Musk. I found no Wall Street Journal analysis behind it (Opinion) [32].
  • Meta's "tens of thousands of idle GPUs" is mostly a design choice: spare chips held in reserve to keep training runs going when others fail (Reported, the Register) [33].

How big is the gap? Nobody publishes chips delivered but not yet powered, at any company. My own rough reconciliation of chip sales against powered capacity puts the stock waiting beyond a normal one to two quarter installation lag at about 4 gigawatts worldwide at the end of 2026, within a range of zero to about 12. The answer depends almost entirely on how long you assume installation takes (Estimate, low confidence) [34].

Why it matters. Chips waiting for power cannot be rented, so warehouses and rising rental prices are not a contradiction. Together they say one of the gates is powered buildings.

The pattern: growth rates are coming down from wild to merely very fast.

  • Google's step from 16 to 22 billion a minute works out to roughly 3.5 times a year if it held (my arithmetic). The step before was faster.
  • Anthropic went from about 2.5 times in one quarter to 1.45 times the next. That is a real slowdown. But 1.45 times a quarter, if it held, is still more than 4 times a year (my arithmetic).
  • On the one platform that publishes the split (OpenRouter), most of the growth came from software agents rather than people (Secondhand).

Three cautions:

  • Token counts overstate work. Agents resend their context at every step, and much of that is served from a cheap saved copy.
  • Spending can stay flat while usage rises, because prices per token keep falling.
  • These are global signals, while the model below is for North America. The model starts from about 250 trillion tokens a day of demand outside China (an estimate, range 190 to 300) and assumes 75% of it is served from North American data centers (an estimate).

6. Lanes: what can actually be built in 2027 and 2028

Spending is not the limit. The five largest builders are expected to spend about $800 billion in 2026 and, by one Goldman Sachs estimate, about $1.2 trillion in 2027 (Secondhand; the note itself is not public) [40]. Amazon alone raised its 2026 plan to about $220 billion (Reported) [11].

Two gates limit what can be built.

  • Gate one, powered buildings:
    • Grid: the auction above cleared at its cap and still fell short.
    • Turbines: the largest maker has about 100 gigawatts booked or reserved worldwide, much of it running to the end of the decade.
    • Transformers: large power transformers took about 128 weeks on average in a 2025 industry survey (Reported, Wood Mackenzie via POWER magazine) [41].
    • Local permission: several US cities and states are debating or applying pauses on large data center permits (Reported) [42].
    • The workaround: more builders are putting gas generation on site instead of waiting for the grid, which loosens this gate at the margin.
  • Gate two, chips and memory:
    • The three memory makers report record profits, and Micron says most of 2027 is committed. Nvidia's $160 billion jump in commitments, mostly memory, is likely the other side of those same contracts, so I count it as one signal, not two.
    • Nvidia has reportedly prepaid at least one supplier, the chip tester King Yuan Electronics (KYEC), to expand testing capacity; the amount and end date are not disclosed (Secondhand) [43].
    • Custom chips from Google, Amazon and others add supply outside Nvidia, but they need the same memory.

How much will come online? Forecasts disagree, mostly because they count different things.

  • SemiAnalysis expects about 43 to 50 gigawatts of new data center capacity worldwide in 2027, with more than 24 gigawatts already in advanced construction (Estimate, partly paywalled) [44].
  • Sightline Climate estimated that 30% to 50% of global data center capacity slated for 2026 is unlikely to come online this year [45]. SemiAnalysis says Sightline's US under construction figures are off by multiples (Estimate, both) [46].
  • Morgan Stanley reportedly sees a US data center power shortfall of about 32 gigawatts, about 34% of need, through 2028, even after on site power and fuel cells (Reported, via Reuters) [47].

8. How I model supply and demand for 2027 and 2028

The model runs eight scenarios against three supply paths for North America. In each scenario, I look for what year will show demand hitting 85% of capacity, which is where I assume rationing and price rises start (a "squeeze"). I also look for what year demand passes 100%, where there will be a real shortage. I've attached the model for transparency (and did my best to make it look nice using AI). Every assumption sits on one inputs sheet, so anyone can change one number and watch it ripple through.

Demand paths (yearly growth in 2027, fading over time):

  • S1 like the early cloud (1.7 times)
  • S2 like the internet after 2001 (2 times)
  • S3 like mobile data (2.7 times)
  • S4 today's pace, then fading (4 times). This is my headline case: conservative and defensible.
  • S8 acceleration, then fading (5 times, then 3.4 and 2.7). This is the faster case I would actually bet on.
  • S5 last year's pace returns (7 times)
  • S6 today's pace holds for three years
  • S7 demand falls

Supply paths. Each one pairs a build rate with an efficiency rate:

  • Low: 6 gigawatts added in 2027, efficiency +21% (roughly what old chips gain from software alone).
  • Base: 12 gigawatts added, efficiency +70%.
  • High: about 20 gigawatts of announced projects less 25% slippage, so about 15 gigawatts added in 2027, efficiency +175%, held back by memory in 2027.

How full is the road today? Here's the part I want to be really upfront about. Nobody publishes how much AI capacity is actually running in North America, or how much work each megawatt really does. Those two numbers decide how full the road is today. Depending on what you assume, it's anywhere from about 36% to 100% full. My central case is about 60% (Estimate): busy, but still moving.

That also shows a test my model fails. Rents, vacancy and what the suppliers are saying all behave as if the road were already full, yet my central case says 60%. Either today's load is higher than I assume, rationing starts well below 85%, or the newest chips are the real bottleneck. I don't know which yet (and I'm not sure anyone does).

How the scenarios work. Think of each scenario as one guess about traffic (a demand path) paired with one guess about lanes (a supply path). Eight demand paths times three supply paths gives 24 possible futures. For each one, the model asks two simple questions: in which year does traffic first pass 85% of the road, and in which year does it pass 100%?

The supply paths are easier to picture than they sound. Low means building slips and chips only get a little better. Base is my central view. High means most announced projects get built and new chips do a lot more work.

Here's how the weights turn into odds. Take every pairing of a demand path and a supply path, and multiply their weights. For example, S4 (25) on Base supply (50) carries 12.5% of the total. Then add up the pairings that cross the 85% line by a given year. That sum is the chance of a squeeze. Do the same with the 100% line and you get the chance of a shortage. Anyone can change the weights on the inputs sheet and watch the odds move.

Let me put that in context:

  • Half the squeeze risk by 2028 comes from supply falling short (the Low path). Most of the rest comes from demand growing faster than today on my central supply path (S5, S6 and S8).
  • My headline case, S4, never squeezes on the central supply path. It stays about two thirds full through 2028, and only squeezes if supply falls short.
  • Efficiency is the swing number. If chips and software improve by +100% next year instead of +70%, the chance of a squeeze by 2028 drops from about 1 in 2 to about 1 in 3.
  • The other big unknown is how much work each megawatt really does today. If it's less than I assume, the chance of a squeeze by 2028 rises to about 7 in 10. If it's more, it falls to about 3 in 10.
  • I hold these odds loosely. They move in jumps, because a few futures sit right on the line.

Big factors that the model depends on

  • The answer depends more on numbers nobody publishes (capacity running today, real work per megawatt, how fast efficiency improves) than on any of the physical constraints.
  • At today's pace, my central case gets a little fuller through 2028. It does not jam.
  • If growth speeds up to about 5 times a year, my central case reaches a shortage in 2028.
  • In the slow demand paths, supply runs far ahead by 2028. Both tails are real.

9. How 2027 and 2028 could play out

There are two strong, and opposite, ways this could go. Here's the best case I can make for each, and then where I land.

Case one: compute becomes scarce in 2028

  1. Usage keeps compounding. Google's model API traffic more than doubled in two quarters [4] [35]. Anthropic's revenue is still growing about 45% a quarter, even after slowing [37].
  2. The open rental market got dearer in 2026, on old and new chips, at more than one provider, after getting cheaper in 2025 [16] [17] [21] [23].
  3. Memory is largely spoken for in 2027, by Micron's own account, and new factory space takes years to build [14].
  4. Powered buildings are scarce. The grid auction cleared at its cap and still fell short [5]. Turbines and transformers are booked years out [6] [41]. Leasable space is 1.4% vacant [7].
  5. Order books are enormous. Microsoft alone holds $678 billion of contracted future revenue [10]. That covers all its commercial products, grew only 25% excluding OpenAI, and the big backlogs overlap through the same few AI labs [12].
  6. Rationing already happens through usage caps and quotas (Reported, mixed sources).
  7. If supply falls short (my Low path), even today's pace of demand becomes a shortage in 2027.

Where this case is weakest:

  • Prices per token for end users keep falling, so scarcity shows up in rentals and rationing, not in list prices.
  • Part of the rental price rise is costs being passed on, not scarcity.
  • My model's central case does not jam at today's pace.
  • The order books lean on a few customers that are burning cash.
  • Record supplier revenue shows buying, not use. The same numbers would appear in an overbuild.

Case two: 2028 brings a glut

  1. Spending far outruns revenue. About $800 billion of 2026 spending [40] against roughly $115 billion a year of combined OpenAI and Anthropic revenue [36] [38]. The comparison is illustrative, since the spending also serves cloud, advertising and internal use. Apollo's chief economist argues operating cash flow must roughly triple, from about $600 billion to about $2 trillion by 2030, for the plans to hold (Verified) [50].
  2. Growth is slowing at the top. Anthropic's quarterly growth fell from about 147% to about 45% (my arithmetic from Reported figures; the third quarter number is unaudited) [36] [37]. OpenAI's run rate is below what it had indicated to investors [38]. Ramp's economist reports token spend among its business customers flat in August and September while token volumes rose, though Ramp's adoption measure picked up in October (Reported) [53].
  3. Demand is concentrated.
    • OpenAI was about 45% of Microsoft's contracted backlog at the end of 2025, by Microsoft's own account (Verified) [51]. Excluding OpenAI, that backlog grew 25% in the year to June (Verified) [12].
    • Anthropic has reportedly committed at least $518 billion to compute over about a decade, about 80% of it payable regardless of use (Reported) [52].
    • Anthropic posted an $80.7 billion first half loss, about $72 billion of it a non cash accounting charge (Reported, from a non public draft) [36].
  4. Some demand is financed by the suppliers. Nvidia has invested in customers and offers some of them payment terms of up to a year. It says its rise in days to collect (45 to 60) comes from "extended payment terms" on large agreements (Verified) [8]. Nobody has measured how much of the backlog this props up.
  5. Credit markets are worried.
    • CoreWeave's five year default insurance rose above 1,000 basis points in mid October (Reported) [54].
    • Oracle's reached 288, up from 260 a week earlier, as technology default insurance hit records (Reported) [54].
    • Oracle sent a force majeure notice to the developer of its New Mexico data center (Project Jupiter, backed by an $18 billion bank loan), Bloomberg reported on 24 September (Reported) [55].
  6. Efficiency could outrun demand. Caching, new chips and custom silicon all cut the compute behind each token [48] [49].
  7. The 2026 and 2027 orders land in 2028, and my slower demand paths leave supply far ahead by then.

Where this case is weakest:

  • No rental price series shows oversupply in 2026.
  • The slowdown evidence is one lab, coming off a very high base.
  • Credit stress pushes out smaller builders, which tightens supply in the near term rather than loosening it (my view; a distressed seller could also dump chips onto the rental market).

Where I land

This is my read, based on the analysis above. I'll be upfront about where the evidence is strong and where it isn't.

  • 2027 looks tight at the margin, and it's the year to watch. Open market rents rose in 2026 on old chips as well as new ones, leasable space is 1.4% vacant, and Micron says most of its 2027 memory is already committed. My model is milder: on my central supply path, my headline case (S4) is about 65% full in 2027, against about 60% today. The chance of a squeeze in 2027 is about 3 in 10, mostly from supply falling short. The gap between what the market shows and what my model says is itself the finding. 2027 will tell us how close the squeeze really is.
  • I don't believe demand goes backwards. I don't see AI use falling off a cliff. The real question is how fast it grows, not whether it grows.
  • If scarcity comes, I think it comes in 2028. The chance of a squeeze by 2028 is about 1 in 2, and of a shortage about 2 in 5. On my central supply path, a 2028 shortage needs growth faster than today's pace: acceleration to about 5 times a year (S8, the case I'd actually bet on) or a return to last year's pace (S5). At today's pace, the road stays about two thirds full.
  • The swing number is efficiency. My central case is +70% in 2027, within a range of about +15% to +175%. Nobody measures it for a real fleet, and it moves the 2028 answer more than anything else in the model.
  • Where I could be wrong: if most of the 2026 rent rise is costs being passed on rather than scarcity, 2027 looks more like my model than like the market. If efficiency lands near +100%, the 2028 odds fall to about 1 in 3.
  • What I would not claim either way: a date when the world runs out of compute, or a count of chips sitting in warehouses. Neither can be measured from public data.
