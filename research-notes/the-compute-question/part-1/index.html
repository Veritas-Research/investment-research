Chinese AI models are cheaper. But what does "cheaper" mean?

You have probably heard that Chinese open models cost a fifth of the price of American frontier models, or less. When DeepSeek launched in January 2025, shares in US power companies fell 20% or more in a day, on the idea that the world would need fewer data centers.

But what does "cheaper" mean? Cheaper to buy? Less electricity? Less computing power? Those are three different questions, and they have three different answers. This guide breaks them down.

Note: A lot of research has gone into checking and referencing the facts in this guide, but it is not perfect and some assumptions have been made. Please treat it as an illustration and do your own research to confirm. I did my best to show how the token counts are measured and where they come from. I also added the calculation method and figures from published lab measurements, good for the right ballpark but not for the last decimal. The method and every source are at the bottom. The views are my own and do not reflect my employer's views or stance.

What models cost: think of a taxi ride

1. The fare per kilometrePrice per token. What the company charges you. It can be set low on purpose, like a taxi firm losing money to win customers.
2. Fuel per kilometreEnergy per token. How much electricity the chip burns to produce one token. This is physics.
3. Kilometres to get thereTokens per task. A driver who takes the long way round uses more fuel even in an efficient car.

Remember: A token is a chunk of a word, about three quarters of one. The power plant only feels fuel per kilometre times kilometres driven. It never sees the fare.

Some models take the long way

Using the taxi analogy above, we can compare how far different models travel to reach the same destination: the right answer. For this I use a study called OckBench (Du, Kang, Han, Krishna and Zhu, "OckBench: Measuring the Efficiency of LLM Reasoning", arXiv 2511.05722, version 3, June 2026). The researchers gave models the same 200 math, coding and science problems, checked which ones each model got right, and counted the tokens each one wrote to get there. Much of it is hidden "thinking out loud". Because every model faced the same problems, models with about the same score can be compared fairly: same destination, different distance.

Fuel: it depends on how much of the model wakes up

A model is a giant grid of numbers called parameters. Bigger grid, more electricity per token. But not in a straight line: one study found 70 times more parameters cost about 7 times more energy per token.

Many models use a trick here. Picture a school with 1,000 teachers. An older design makes every teacher read your question. A "mixture of experts" model sends it to only the 30 who know the subject. Measured saving: about 3.6 times less energy per token against an older design of the same total size, and less when the model is very large.

This is not a Chinese trick. OpenAI's open model (gpt-oss) uses it, so does France's Mixtral, and so do DeepSeek, Qwen and Kimi. The closed labs do not publish their designs, so nobody outside knows how much of GPT, Claude, Gemini or Grok wakes up per token. Kimi, Qwen and DeepSeek publish it. Kimi K2 has 1 trillion parameters and wakes about 32 billion. The newer Kimi K3 has 2.8 trillion and wakes about 104 billion.

Caching: why token counts overstate the load

Picture one server and two workers. The first asks a question and the server does the full work. The second asks the same thing a moment later and the server hands back a saved copy. The token counter records two requests' worth of tokens. The electricity meter records barely more than one.

This is called caching, and coding agents lean on it constantly. At every step an agent resends the whole conversation so far: the files it has read, the plan, the results. Almost all of that was already processed a moment ago, so the server reuses its saved work instead of redoing it. It is the difference between rereading your notes and writing new ones.

The effect on the numbers is large. Company token counts, the ones quoted in headlines, add up every token, saved copies included. So as people move from chat to agents, token counts grow much faster than the real work the chips are doing.

Weights come from what the labs charge: new output 1, fresh input 0.2, saved copy 0.02. Price is used as a rough guide to compute. No lab publishes the true ratio.

Measured on real coding tasks: an agent used about 4.17 million tokens per task against 3,400 for a back and forth coding chat. That is 1,200 times the tokens but only about 80 times the cost (my division of the paper's $1.86 and $0.023 per task). The gap is saved copies. In one company's data (Jellyfish), 96% of coding agent tokens were cache reads. A second data set (Requesty) puts cache reads at 46% to 92% of input tokens, depending on the tool.

Caching is not free. A long saved context still occupies chip memory, and writing each new token gets slower as the context behind it grows.

So when we talk about "cheaper", it is really a claim about price. A cheaper price per token sometimes lowers the customer's bill and sometimes does not: in this test Kimi K2.6 cost 3.5 times more per problem than GPT-5.5 on low, at the same score. On hard problems it does not clearly lower the work the data center has to do: the cheaper models tested reach the same answer by writing far more tokens, and how big the US models are is unknown.

So why is it so much cheaper? Because price per token is a business decision. It reflects competition, what a company is willing to lose to win users, and how hard it packs its servers. A chip rental company estimated that renting chips to run Kimi yourself costs about $3.10 to $5.20 per million tokens, while its maker charged about $1.56 (a blend of its input and output prices). A lab running its own chips at full load pays far less, so this does not show the maker loses money. It shows the price of a token reflects strategy and scale. It does not tell you how much electricity the token used.

So what are the implications? If the world switched to cheaper models wherever they do the job well enough, it might save money, and on the evidence so far it would not clearly save computing power on hard reasoning work. Short everyday answers have not been measured. Cheaper tokens also tend to get used more, so cheaper models are unlikely to shrink the need for chips, data centers and electricity. The pressure lands on the companies selling tokens, whose prices fall, and not on the demand for compute.

Where I am going with this next. Whichever models the world picks, American or Chinese, the data centers still have to be built. Methodology: how the energy figures are built

Disputes are welcome. Here is every step, so you can check it or swap in your own numbers with the dials.

Formula: energy per task = 0.15 x (awake parameters in billions / 32)^0.47 x multipliers x tokens. The power 0.47 can be moved up to 1 with the dial under the comparison.

One sanity check and two figures for scale. The method gives about 1.3 watt hours for a top model on medium thinking; the same lab study that supplies the starting number measured an average of about 1.3 watt hours (4,625 joules, chips only) per reasoning answer, averaged across all the models it tested. That is a sanity check, not an independent one. Microsoft researchers estimated a median 0.31 watt hours per ordinary query on frontier models (over 200 billion parameters) (arXiv 2509.20241); Google reports 0.24.

Known weak points. The size rule rests on two measured points from one model family, covers 1 to 70 billion parameters only, and is the low end: another study measured a power of 0.91. Counting only awake parameters is a best case, because the whole model still sits in chip memory. Closed labs do not publish model sizes, so "100 billion awake" for a top model and "10 billion" for a fast one are placeholders. Every token count comes from one benchmark of 200 math, coding and science problems. Its authors picked the problems where models differ most in how much they write, so the gaps here are the wide end, not the average. Each model answered once at a fixed setting; the paper reports no repeat runs. Agent work and everyday chat were not tested. Papers on arXiv are preprints and not peer reviewed unless noted. Each number was reopened at its source by a second checker, and each still rests on a single source.
