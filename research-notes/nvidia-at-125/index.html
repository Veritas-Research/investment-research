NVIDIA at $125: Is the Business Still Solid, and Is the Stock Undervalued?

Series: Research notes (concepts I explore). Not a position piece.
Prices: as of the November 20, 2026 close, the last trading day before publication.

The views and analysis here are my own and have nothing to do with my employer. This is research, not investment advice. I have no direct position in NVIDIA; I hold it only through broad market index funds. Several numbers below are my own estimates, and I label them as estimates. Treat them as ranges in the right ballpark, not forecasts to the decimal.

Take away: in two minutes

  • Where it stands: NVIDIA closed at $125.53 on November 20, down 14% that day and nearly 48% below its record close of $239.24 on October 6 (Reported) [20] [21] [24].
  • How solid is the business? Very, on the numbers. Revenue last quarter was $115.8 billion, up 103% from a year ago, and NVIDIA guided to $130 billion for the next quarter (Verified) [1]. No official or outlet I found alleges NVIDIA misstated its revenue. But two piles of revenue could go away: China linked sales and sales to customers NVIDIA itself funds or backs. I put that at 5% to 22% of revenue (Estimate) [2] [26].
  • The open question: on November 20, Reuters reported that Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) enforcement staff are examining potential accounting issues at NVIDIA. The sources are anonymous, and nobody yet knows which practices (Reported) [22].
  • What the market is assuming: at $125, NVIDIA trades at about 8 times the Street's earnings forecast for next year. Working backwards with Aswath Damodaran's own valuation spreadsheet, the price only works if sales shrink for years after next year, or the operating margin falls to about a third of sales, half of last quarter's 67% (Estimate) [26] [28].
  • What it's worth: about $199 a share if growth cools and margins slip to 57%. A 3 year check that ignores the far future gives $185 to $235 for the same story. Across all 24 combinations I tested, not one lands below $125.53 (Estimate) [26].
  • Undervalued or overvalued? On the numbers I can check, undervalued. The one thing the model can't price is whether the starting revenue is right, and that's what the accounting inquiry and NVIDIA's next quarterly filing will tell us.

What is going on

In six weeks NVIDIA lost roughly $2.7 trillion of market value (Reported) [24]. Its founder and CEO, Jensen Huang, resigned on November 12. The government is looking at its China sales, Congress wants answers, and now the SEC is reportedly looking at its accounting. Meanwhile it just reported the biggest quarter in its history.

That gap bothered me. Either the market knows something the income statement doesn't show yet, or it's panicking.

So I didn't want another story about NVIDIA. I wanted the mechanics. Aswath Damodaran, a finance professor at New York University's Stern School of Business, publishes his valuation spreadsheets openly, and on November 19 he published one for NVIDIA. I took his spreadsheet engine, put in NVIDIA's latest numbers, and asked three questions:

  1. How solid is the revenue underneath the business?
  2. What is today's price already assuming?
  3. If I make my own assumptions, what does the same machine say NVIDIA is worth?

An analogy I'll use throughout

I love analogies; they help me picture complex subjects.

  • NVIDIA is an apartment building that just doubled its rent roll. Rent is revenue.
  • Most tenants are big, solid companies (the large cloud providers) paying their own way.
  • Some tenants received investment from the landlord and then signed leases. That's NVIDIA buying stakes in its customers.
  • The landlord also backstops some tenants' leases elsewhere. Those are NVIDIA's guarantees.
  • One group of tenants may be evicted by the city. That's the China linked revenue facing new export rules.
  • The building's price today implies rents will fall for years. That's the assumption I want to test.

Part 2How solid is the business?

If the sales are inflated, none of the valuation math matters. So I checked this first.

Can the buyers afford it?

The five biggest buyers (Amazon, Alphabet, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle) spent about $237 billion on buildings and equipment last quarter. NVIDIA's sales to large clouds were $60.1 billion (Verified, with two caveats: Microsoft's figure includes finance leases and is Reported, about $231 billion using its cash figure instead; Oracle's quarter ended August 31) [1] [8]. So the buyers can easily afford what NVIDIA says they bought.

That's all this check proves. Most NVIDIA chips reach the clouds through server makers, and NVIDIA says its view of the end customer "is an estimation" (Verified) [3]. Spending totals can't verify how NVIDIA sorts its customers.

Two piles of revenue that could go away

Pile 1: China.

  • NVIDIA's own filing puts revenue from customers headquartered in China at $11.05 billion last quarter, up from $2.97 billion a year earlier (Verified) [2].
  • $2.4 billion of that was licensed chips that NVIDIA has now stopped shipping (Verified) [1] [4].
  • The other $8.65 billion isn't broken down. NVIDIA's entire non data center business (gaming, PCs and other edge chips) was $8.2 billion worldwide. Even if every dollar of that went to Chinese companies, which it didn't, at least $0.45 billion would still be data center chips. The real number is probably far higher, but the filing doesn't say (Verified figures [1] [2]; the inference is mine).
  • A Commerce Department rule took effect on November 19 requiring licenses to supply AI chips to data centers anywhere that may serve Chinese companies. It also adds licensing for Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Thailand and Brazil, and gives existing arrangements 90 days to get licensed (Reported, from law firm summaries) [12].

Pile 2: sales NVIDIA helped pay for.

  • NVIDIA bought $15.7 billion of stakes in other companies last quarter, and $58.1 billion over nine months, including a one time $30 billion into OpenAI (Verified) [1] [18].
  • It has guaranteed up to $118 billion, mostly $105 billion of residual value guarantees on an Ohio data center campus leased to OpenAI. If OpenAI can't pay, NVIDIA covers the shortfall, and OpenAI must pay NVIDIA back. NVIDIA says it has made no payments, and the guarantees start phase by phase from fiscal 2029 (Verified) [1] [5].
  • Customers are taking longer to pay: 64 days on average, up from 60. NVIDIA says these are longer terms for "certain investment-grade customers" (Verified) [1].
  • NVIDIA's position, from a memo to analysts a year ago: it "does not rely on vendor financing arrangements to grow revenue." Back then its strategic investments were about $3.7 billion a quarter; last quarter they were $15.7 billion (Reported) [1] [7].
  • In the apartment analogy, this is the risk that some rent ultimately traces back to the landlord's own money. It's real cash today, but some of it lasts only as long as the landlord keeps investing.

The investigations

  • Reported, not confirmed: The Wall Street Journal reported on November 17 that the SEC opened a civil probe examining, in part, whether NVIDIA's disclosures misled investors (Reported) [16]. On November 20, Reuters reported that SEC enforcement staff are examining potential accounting issues; it couldn't say which practices or when the inquiry began. The SEC declined to comment (Reported) [22].
  • On the record: Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent publicly asked whether NVIDIA pushed "seemingly illicit sales" to lift revenue (Reported) [17].
  • Confirmed: the Justice Department and Commerce Department are investigating Megaspeed, a Singapore customer, for potential export violations. Their statement doesn't describe NVIDIA as a target (Verified) [13].
  • For context: the SEC brought only 16 actions against public companies in fiscal 2026, the fewest on record (Reported) [14]. NVIDIA paid $5.5 million in 2022 to settle an earlier disclosure case, and nothing was restated (Verified) [15].
  • What I'd watch: NVIDIA has not filed its quarterly report (10-Q) yet. In recent quarters it filed on results day (Reported) [25]. The deadline is about December 4. A late filing would worry me far more than any headline.

My take: I found no evidence that NVIDIA misstated its revenue. I did find a lot of revenue that depends on China staying open and on NVIDIA continuing to fund its customers. That's a dependability problem, not a finding of misstated revenue. The accounting inquiry could change that, and I'll update this piece if it does.

Part 3How Damodaran values a company

Story first, then numbers, and every number can be changed.

Damodaran's method is simple to describe and hard to fake. He writes a short story about the company, then turns each part of the story into one number:

  1. How big sales get (revenue growth).
  2. How much of each sales dollar becomes profit (operating margin).
  3. How much the company must reinvest to grow (dollars of new sales per dollar invested).
  4. How much future money is discounted (the cost of capital, about 10.55% for chip companies in his January 2026 data) (Verified) [9].
  5. The chance the company fails.
  6. Then the bridge to shareholders: add cash and stakes in other companies, subtract debt, divide by shares.

He shows his work and changes it in public. The Anthropic spreadsheet he shared values the company at $1.45 trillion, and on November 18 he revised his per share value to $76 as new facts arrived (Verified) [10]. On November 19 he valued NVIDIA at about $4.8 trillion, or roughly $200 a share, with a breakeven of about $140 where he has a buy order. His valuation is dated October 29, before the resignation and the latest quarter (Verified) [11].

I rebuilt his spreadsheet in code to make sure I understood it. It reproduces his Anthropic answer to the dollar. Then I put in NVIDIA's latest numbers (Estimate, my model) [26].

Is that crazy? Not necessarily. The bears have argued some version of this since AI took off: customers are building their own chips, and some of the spending is circular, with NVIDIA investing in companies that then buy its chips (Opinion, the bear case). But it's a specific bet, and now we can see how big it is.

The middle case in plain numbers

  1. Sales: $543 billion next year, about $633 billion by year 3.
  2. Margin: 63% in year 1 (below last quarter's 67%), 59% by year 3, 57% by year 5.
  3. Earnings per share: about $11.70, $12.10 and $12.90 over the next three years. That's below the Street's $15.85, partly because I assume margins slip.

All Estimate, my model [26]. Damodaran's method then values every future year of that cash, which gives $199. One honest caveat: more than half of that comes from years after 2036, which is why I ran a second check that doesn't need them.

Take away: undervalued or overvalued?

What the analysis shows, and why I think it holds up.

On every set of assumptions I tested, NVIDIA is worth more than $125.53. Here's why I think that's defensible:

  • No combination lands below the price. I tested 24: two growth stories, three long run margins and four levels of revenue lost for good. The lowest is $128, and it requires all three bad outcomes at once (Estimate) [26].
  • The 3 year check agrees. Ignoring everything after 2029, the middle story is worth $185 to $235. Only the gloomy story at a below average multiple gets down to $125 (Estimate) [26].
  • To justify $125 you have to believe something extreme. About 8 times next year's expected earnings, sales shrinking for years after next year, or a long run margin of about a third of sales, half of what NVIDIA earned last quarter (Estimate) [26] [28].
  • Damodaran and the Street both land higher. His value of about $200 and his breakeven of about $140 sit above today's price, and so do the targets of the banks that downgraded the stock (Verified [11]; Secondhand [30]).
  • The scary balance sheet items barely move the answer. Guarantees, slower payments and rent back commitments each take $2 to $5 off the value (Estimate) [26].

Where this could be wrong:

  • The starting number. Everything above assumes the $361.8 billion of revenue is real. If the SEC finds revenue was booked improperly, the base changes and the whole valuation has to be redone. The model can't price that, and I think it's the main reason the stock trades where it does.
  • Margins below my grid. If customers' own chips take most of the market, margins could fall below 45%. At 33%, $125 is fair even with growth intact.
  • The long tail. Damodaran's method leans on years after 2036 for more than half of its value. The 3 year check lands in the same range without them, but it depends on the multiple the market is willing to pay in 2029.

My read: on the numbers we can see, the stock looks undervalued. The middle of my range is about $185 to $235, and the gloomy end sits right at today's price. The gap between price and value is the market charging for doubt about the revenue itself. The 10-Q is the first real test of that doubt, and I'll come back with a follow up when it lands.

Methodology

I looked at company filings and releases, call transcripts, news reports and Damodaran's published work myself. To bring it together I used AI agents to fetch and organise the material, a separate hostile audit that rechecked 100 claims against their original documents and forced a series of corrections, swaps and cuts, and a code rebuild of Damodaran's spreadsheet to check the math. A few claims could only be confirmed through secondhand sources and are labelled that way. Two simplifications in the model: I value NVIDIA's stakes in other companies at book value ($109.7 billion), and I don't treat research spending as an investment. The model is published alongside this piece [26].
